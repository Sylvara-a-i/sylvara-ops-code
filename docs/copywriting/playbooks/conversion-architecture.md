# Conversion Architecture Playbook

## Purpose

Organize an offer and its supporting copy so the reader can evaluate relevance, value, proof, risk, and next steps without pressure or confusion.

## Offer Before Copy

Copy cannot repair an incoherent offer. Define:

- target audience and qualifying situation;
- desired outcome and scope;
- deliverables;
- delivery process and timeline;
- customer responsibilities;
- price, billing, renewal, and cancellation terms;
- support and service boundaries;
- proof and material limitations;
- guarantee or risk-reversal policy, if any;
- capacity, availability, and operational constraints; and
- next step and what happens after it.

If these are unclear, improve the offer before increasing copy intensity.

## Value Architecture

Describe value through four lenses:

1. **Outcome value:** What useful change can the offer help create?
2. **Process value:** What effort, delay, coordination, or uncertainty can it reduce?
3. **Confidence value:** What evidence, expertise, controls, or transparency reduce perceived risk?
4. **Fit value:** Why is this appropriate for this audience, workflow, environment, or constraint?

Do not create fictional monetary value or inflate line items to manufacture a discount.

## Offer Stack

Present components in decision order:

- core result or service;
- essential deliverables;
- implementation or onboarding;
- support;
- optional additions;
- price and terms;
- eligibility and limitations; and
- next step.

Bonuses should improve the customer's ability to achieve the core result. Unrelated extras add cognitive load and support cost.

## Proof Architecture

Match the proof to the question:

| Reader question | Suitable proof |
|---|---|
| Does it work as described? | Demonstration, product evidence, process evidence, or approved results |
| Will it work in my situation? | Relevant case evidence, fit criteria, constraints, or comparison |
| Can this team deliver? | Approved credentials, operating method, reliability controls, or references |
| Is the claim believable? | Specific mechanism, transparent limitations, source evidence, or independent validation |
| Is the decision safe? | Clear terms, security controls, support boundaries, pilot, cancellation, or approved guarantee |

Proof should appear near the claim it supports. A long testimonial wall cannot rescue an unsupported core promise.

## Objection System

Group objections by underlying risk:

- **Need:** Is the problem important enough?
- **Fit:** Is this designed for my situation?
- **Trust:** Is the provider and claim credible?
- **Effort:** Will adoption create more work than it removes?
- **Time:** How long before value, and what is required now?
- **Money:** Is the cost justified and affordable?
- **Authority:** Who else must approve?
- **Technical risk:** Will it integrate, remain secure, and fail safely?
- **Switching risk:** What happens to current workflows and data?
- **Opportunity cost:** What will this displace?

Resolve objections with facts, process, proof, scope, or a better offer. Do not mock, shame, or dismiss reasonable concerns.

## Risk Reversal

Risk reversal may include:

- a clear pilot;
- phased implementation;
- milestone acceptance;
- transparent cancellation;
- bounded scope;
- reversible setup;
- proof before expansion;
- an approved refund or service guarantee; or
- clear success and stop criteria.

The copy must match the actual operational policy. Never promise a guarantee that finance, operations, product, or customer support cannot honor.

## Urgency And Scarcity

Use urgency only when a real time-dependent condition exists. Use scarcity only when a real capacity or inventory constraint exists.

Acceptable sources include a verified event date, enrollment window, delivery capacity, regulatory deadline, expiring approved price, or finite inventory. Record the authoritative source and read it back before publication.

General desire for faster sales is not evidence of urgency.

## Call To Action

A strong call to action states:

- the action;
- the immediate value or purpose;
- what information or commitment is required;
- what happens next;
- timing or response expectations; and
- any relevant eligibility or cost.

Use one primary call to action per decision stage. Secondary actions should serve readers who are not ready without competing equally with the primary action.

## Page Sequence

A conversion page commonly needs to answer these questions:

1. Is this relevant to me?
2. What useful outcome is offered?
3. Why is the current problem or alternative insufficient?
4. How does this approach work?
5. Why should I believe it?
6. What exactly do I receive?
7. What does it require or cost?
8. What risks, objections, and limitations matter?
9. What should I do next?

Sequence follows reader questions, not a fixed template. Ready buyers may need a short path. Complex, unfamiliar, or high-risk offers require more explanation and proof.

## Long-Form Architecture

Use long form only when the decision complexity justifies it. A controlled structure is:

- relevant lead;
- problem and stakes;
- insight or differentiated mechanism;
- solution and process;
- proof;
- offer and deliverables;
- objections;
- terms and risk;
- call to action;
- concise close or summary.

Every section must earn its length by changing understanding, belief, or readiness. Repetition without new proof or clarity is not persuasion.

## SaaS And Service Architecture

For SaaS or productized services, make these elements easy to find:

- primary use case;
- audience and environment fit;
- product or service workflow;
- key capabilities and operational effects;
- integrations and prerequisites;
- security or reliability boundaries when relevant;
- proof;
- pricing or buying path;
- onboarding and support; and
- clear next step.

Do not advertise an integration, automation, security control, or support level that is only planned or proposed.

## Conversion Friction Audit

Check for:

- unclear audience or outcome;
- multiple competing calls to action;
- hidden price, terms, or next steps;
- form fields that are unnecessary at this stage;
- proof far from the claim;
- unresolved implementation or switching risk;
- vague deliverables;
- excessive navigation or repeated sections;
- mobile-unfriendly length or formatting;
- inaccessible forms or controls; and
- promises that require explanation only after purchase.

## Validation

- The offer is operationally real and commercially current.
- Value is explained without invented monetary arithmetic.
- Proof and consent support the claims made.
- Objections are answered with substance.
- Guarantees, urgency, and scarcity match authoritative state.
- Price, terms, delivery, support, and limitations are visible.
- The primary call to action is clear and proportionate to the reader's readiness.
